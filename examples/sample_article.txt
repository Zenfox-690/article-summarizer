Community gardens can improve access to fresh food while giving neighbors a shared place to learn and work. A two-year study across twelve neighborhoods found that gardens with regular volunteer schedules produced more vegetables and stayed active longer than gardens relying on occasional events. Organizers said the most useful supports were secure water access, a small budget for tools, and one named coordinator per site. Researchers cautioned that gardens are not a substitute for broader food programs, but concluded they can strengthen local food networks when residents help plan and maintain them.
